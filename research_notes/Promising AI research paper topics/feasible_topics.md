# Feasible AI research paper topics for a student or independent researcher (as of 25 October 2026)

Scope: paper ideas that can start now, finish in about 3–6 months, and stay within one consumer or single-cloud GPU (or CPU-only), public data or a small self-collected set, and no frontier-model pretraining. No exploit, malware, or bioweapon topics. Citations below are pages that were opened or returned with substantive text. Where a number comes only from an abstract, a company blog, or an un-reviewed preprint, that is stated.

Date constraint: today is 25 October 2026. Several 2025–2026 workshop deadlines cited below have already passed. A project started now cannot target those calls unless a later cycle is announced. No 2027 call for papers was retrieved.

## Which recent papers (2024–2026) explicitly leave open problems that do not require training a frontier model from scratch?

### Takeaway
Several 2025–2026 papers and workshop calls leave evaluation, audit, and small-model problems that do not require pretraining a frontier model: contamination-resistant benchmarks, controlled small-model comparisons, agent memory and tool-use failures, benchmark validity bugs, and single-GPU inference measurement. Some of those “open” problems are only open at long context or across many proprietary systems, which a one-GPU project cannot fully rerun.

### Cited Findings
- Sun et al. tested 10 LLMs, 5 benchmarks, 20 benchmark-data-contamination mitigation strategies, and 2 contamination scenarios. No existing strategy balanced fidelity and contamination resistance. No semantic-preserving strategy significantly beat the unmodified benchmark on resistance across all benchmarks; semantic-altering strategies traded fidelity for resistance. They say this shows a need for more effective mitigation strategies. Code is public. The paper is in the ICML 2025 proceedings (PMLR volume 267). — [Sun et al., PMLR 267](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/sun25t.html); preprint [arXiv:2503.16402](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16402)
- Chen et al., an EMNLP 2025 main-conference survey (pages 10080–10098), say static benchmarks grow more contamination-prone as training sets grow, while dynamic benchmarks lack standardized criteria and often do not measure computational overhead, robustness, or interpretability. They name future work as standardized criteria (correctness, diversity, scalability), contamination-resilient construction (temporal filters, synthetic data, rule-based generation), continual updates, human-in-the-loop validation, public update logs, and extension to multimodal models. — [ACL Anthology 2025.emnlp-main.511](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.511/); discussion text [arXiv HTML 2502.17521v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17521v2)
- Gupta et al. (about 160 papers; arXiv HTML version states that models released after early 2026 may be missing) list open SLM challenges where small models have not shown competitive performance: long-context reasoning beyond 32K tokens, complex multi-step planning, and robustness to adversarial inputs. They also say safety evaluation of SLMs (hallucination rates, bias, harmful-content generation) is underdeveloped, and that multilingual SLM capability is insufficiently evaluated because most benchmarks are English. They say claims that SLMs rival larger models are cross-paper comparisons, not head-to-head runs under one protocol, and that they did not independently verify technical-report numbers. The survey is not a PRISMA review. They separately state that 8-bit Adam training of 7B models on a single RTX 4090 (24 GB) has been reported, citing another paper; that training recipe was not re-checked here. — [arXiv HTML 2501.05465v2, §4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05465v2)
- Corradini, Leonesi, and Piangerelli systematically reviewed 70 English SLM studies from January 2023 to January 2025 (models up to 7B). Counting explicit challenge statements, they report generalization in 25% of papers, data availability in 21%, hallucinations in 20%, scalability in 19%, and interpretability in 15%. Later in the same article they discuss specialized hallucination benchmarks, knowledge distillation aimed at truthfulness, contrastive decoding, test-time scaling, and human-in-the-loop interpretability as directions. Those later paragraphs mix the systematic set with a purposive look at preprints, so the 1B-versus-405B test-time-scaling anecdote in that section was not traced to its primary experiment here and should not be treated as verified. — [Big Data and Cognitive Computing 2025, 9(7), 189](https://doi.org/10.3390/bdcc9070189)
- An IEEE Xplore abstract for a February 2026 survey (IEMENTech; DOI 10.1109/IEMENTech202669403.2026.11434122) says on-device SLMs face open problems of evaluation reliability, safety alignment under resource constraints, and hardware fragmentation, and lists future directions of multimodal on-device SLMs, long-context efficiency, and standardized energy-aware benchmarks. Only the abstract was available, not the full paper. — [IEEE DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/iementech202669403.2026.11434122)
- Hu, Wang, and McAuley introduce MemoryAgentBench (arXiv HTML 2507.05257v2; conference acceptance was not confirmed). They define four memory competencies: accurate retrieval, test-time learning, long-range understanding, and selective forgetting. They report that every method they tried scored at most 7% on multi-hop selective forgetting, while long-context agents did better on single-hop. On a shorter check, o4-mini scored 80.0 on 6K-token FactConsolidation multi-hop and 14.0 at 32K. Their own future-work sentence is narrower: budget limits meant they evaluated only some agents, and they want more agents under the same protocol. Contexts in the benchmark go up to 262K tokens. — [arXiv HTML 2507.05257v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05257v2)
- Wang et al. (arXiv:2505.13328, 19 May 2025; venue not confirmed) propose DialogTool and VirtualMobile for stateful, multi-turn tool use across tool creation, utilization, and role-consistent response. After evaluating 13 open and closed models, they say state-of-the-art LLMs still do not use tools well over long horizons. — [arXiv:2505.13328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13328)
- Zhu et al. (arXiv:2507.02825; venue not confirmed) report that task-setup or reward bugs in agent benchmarks can under- or overestimate performance by up to 100% in relative terms. Concrete cases they state: SWE-bench Verified can pass incomplete tests; a trivial empty-response agent scores 38% on τ-bench and beats a GPT-4o agent on intentionally impossible tasks; an agent can score 100% on SWE-Lancer without resolving tasks; KernelBench overestimates correct kernel generation by 31 absolute points because fuzz testing is incomplete; WebArena string matching overestimates by 5.2 points. Applying their Agentic Benchmark Checklist to CVE-Bench reduced overestimation by 33 absolute points. They release a checklist, code, and a website. CVE-Bench is a cybersecurity benchmark; it is cited here only as their case study, not as a recommended student topic. — [arXiv:2507.02825](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02825)
- UltraHorizon (arXiv:2509.21766v1, 26 September 2025) says most agent benchmarks are short-horizon (thousands of tokens and under about 30 tool calls). Their long-horizon setting averages more than 35K tokens and 60 tool calls, and the heaviest setting averages 200K+ tokens and 400+ tool calls. They report that LLM agents underperform humans, that simple scaling fails, and that trajectory errors fall into eight types tied to in-context locking and missing fundamental capabilities. Full text was a PDF extract; conference acceptance was not confirmed. — [arXiv PDF 2509.21766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.21766)
- Zhao et al., ICPP 2026 (published 27 September 2026), argue that single-GPU LLM inference still lacks traceable cross-layer attribution and dynamic KV-cache characterization. Their experiments used one NVIDIA A100 80GB PCIe, two Xeon Platinum 8358 CPUs, 504 GB RAM, Ubuntu 22.04, CUDA 12.4, vLLM 0.8.5, BF16, and Nsight Compute, on dense and MoE models. That is one datacenter GPU, not a 24 GB consumer card, and not a claim that the same MoE models fit a consumer GPU. — [ACM DL abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3832810.3832894)
- The NeurIPS 2025 time-series foundation-model workshop states that carefully designed lightweight supervised baselines often match time-series foundation models, that these models still need full fine-tuning to be competitive, and that public benchmarks disagree: GIFT-Eval favors foundation models, OpenTS finds statistical models ahead of deep learning on univariate data, and FoundTS finds supervised baselines on par with foundation models. This is the workshop’s motivation, not a re-analysis of those three benchmarks. — [NeurIPS 2025 workshop listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/loc/san-diego/events/workshop)
- The NeurIPS 2025 Efficient Reasoning workshop call asked for resource-constrained data pipelines, efficiency-and-efficacy benchmarks, training and inference methods that trade compute for accuracy, and reasoning on commodity GPUs. Submission deadlines were in September–October 2025. — [Workshop site](https://efficient-reasoning.github.io/)
- Gupta et al. cite Song et al., PowerInfer, SOSP 2024, as fast LLM serving on a consumer-grade GPU. That is a 2024 systems result, not a 2026 open-problem statement. — cited inside [arXiv HTML 2501.05465v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05465v2)

### Inferences
- The open problems that best match a first paper are measurement problems: whether a mitigation, a memory policy, a reward function, a quantization choice, or a hardware bottleneck behaves as claimed on public models. They do not require a new pretraining run.
- “Still open” is time-stamped. MemoryAgentBench’s multi-hop failure and Sun et al.’s negative mitigation result are 2025 findings. Nothing retrieved shows they were closed by 25 October 2026, and nothing retrieved proves they are still unsolved after later 2026 models.
- A head-to-head comparison of open 1B–8B models under one harness directly answers Gupta et al.’s complaint about cross-paper comparisons without calling a frontier API.
- UltraHorizon and the 262K slice of MemoryAgentBench describe real gaps, but the gap is defined at a scale that is a different project from a one-GPU study. The feasible slice is the short-context or static-audit version, and that slice may already be partly solved (o4-mini scores 80 on 6K FactConsolidation multi-hop).

### Gaps
- No faculty blog or university course archive that cites a specific 2024–2026 paper and a student-scale follow-up was retrieved. Those sources were requested and not found in this pass.
- Conference acceptance was not confirmed for MemoryAgentBench, DialogTool, UltraHorizon, or the Agentic Benchmark Checklist paper. They are preprints with public claims, not verified proceedings papers.
- The IEEE on-device survey’s open problems are abstract-only. The body was not read.
- Gupta et al. warn that their own performance table is not independently verified. Secondary model write-ups inside that survey (domain SLMs, effective-size equivalences) were not used as facts.
- Whether selective forgetting at 32K–262K is still unsolved for late-2026 open models was not re-measured.
- ICLR 2027, ACL 2027, and NeurIPS 2026/2027 submission dates were not retrieved, so a 3–6 month calendar cannot be tied to a specific future deadline.

## What kinds of contributions are still accepted with modest compute: new evaluations, error analyses, data audits, small-model methods, human studies, systems measurements, negative results, replications?

### Takeaway
Modest-compute contribution types are explicitly in scope at TMLR and at the 2026 negative-results workshops, and 2025 main-conference papers already consist of evaluations, checklists, and negative mitigation studies. As of 25 October 2026 the cited workshop deadlines have passed, so the rolling venue with written acceptance rules is TMLR. A plain rerun is not enough there. A replication needs a robustness finding, a new baseline, or an explanation of when the original claim breaks.

### Cited Findings
- TMLR’s scope includes experimental studies of how learning systems behave, application papers that show strengths and weaknesses, new tasks and assessment methods, analysis and visualization, and “reproducibility studies of previously published results or claims.” Acceptance asks only whether claims are supported by clear evidence and whether some of the audience would care. The policy says papers can be accepted when the contribution is modest. It says not to reject for lack of state-of-the-art numbers or for a method that is not novel. A class-project rerun that does not surface a generalizable insight can still be rejected for lack of interest. Reproducibility Certification requires added value beyond verification: baselines, ablations, analysis, or insights. TMLR does not accept expanded versions of the authors’ own archival conference papers. — [TMLR editorial policies](https://jmlr.csail.mit.edu/tmlr/editorial-policies.html); [acceptance criteria](https://www.jmlr.org/tmlr/acceptance-criteria.html); [reviewer guide](https://jmlr.csail.mit.edu/tmlr/reviewer-guide.html)
- TMLR’s FAQ says replications of previous research are welcome. — [TMLR FAQ](https://jmlr.csail.mit.edu/tmlr/faq.html)
- A June 2024 position paper argues that machine learning treats “beats the state of the art” as the only good result, distinguishes novel-method failures from failures of existing methods, and asks venues to incentivize replication studies. It points to Pineau et al. (2021) on a reproducibility crisis. This is an argument, not a count of accepted papers. — [arXiv HTML 2406.03980v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.03980v1)
- ICLR 2026’s author guide asks for a reproducibility statement and encourages code, but the guide that was retrieved does not announce an ICLR 2026 reproducibility challenge. The challenges found by name were ICLR 2018 and ICLR 2019. — [ICLR 2026 Author Guide](https://iclr.cc/Conferences/2026/AuthorGuide); [ICLR 2018 challenge](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~jpineau/ICLR2018-ReproducibilityChallenge.html); [ICLR 2019 challenge repo](https://github.com/reproducibility-challenge/iclr_2019/)
- The ICLR 2026 workshop “I Can’t Believe It’s Not Better: Where Large Language Models need to improve” (27 April 2026) invited two kinds of papers: studies that demonstrate a limitation, and attempts that failed, with failure modes and boundary conditions. Named topics were reasoning, alignment, agents, efficiency and scaling under real compute limits (including energy), and hallucinations, plus diagnostics, replications, counterfactual evaluations, and ablations. The call says academic compute limits are a reason negative results go unpublished. Submission deadline was 31 January 2026. — [Workshop home](https://sites.google.com/view/icbinb-2026/home); [ICLR virtual page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/workshop/10000772)
- The ICLR 2025 ICBINB workshop used the same negative-results framing for applied deep learning failures in deployment, including healthcare, scientific discovery, robotics, education, and fairness. That call is also past. — [ICLR 2025 workshop page](https://www.iclr.cc/virtual/2025/workshop/23987)
- Insights from Negative Results in NLP, co-located with EMNLP on 29 October 2026, asked for negative or unexpected results with implications. The call’s examples map directly onto modest compute: recommendations about what not to do when training, fine-tuning, or prompting; ablations that contradict an original paper; datasets or probes that break a prior method; trivial baselines that work too well; language-specific failures; instability across hardware, seeds, or preprocessing; theory for why a method should fail; audits of data collection and annotation; metric failures including LLM-as-judge; policy noncompliance; unforeseen agent behavior; under-reporting and test-set contamination. Short papers were up to 4 pages. Direct submission deadline was extended to 8 June 2026. ARR commitment was 25 June 2026. The workshop date is four days after this note’s date, so a new project cannot submit to this cycle. — [Insights 2026 CFP](https://insights-workshop.github.io/2026/cfp)
- HEAL at CHI 2026 invited human-centered LLM evaluation and auditing: stakeholder needs, methods, tools, policy, and ethics, plus a theme on AI agents used as auditors. Submission types included in-progress empirical studies, method papers, and literature reviews, at 2–6 pages. The page did not, in the retrieved text, state a compute expectation. — [HEAL@CHI 2026](https://heal-workshop.github.io/)
- A CHI 2026 workshop on LLMs as simulated research participants asked for 1–4 page position papers on when LLM stand-ins for interviews, surveys, and usability tests are valid, and how to document them. Risks named in the extended abstract include bias, reproducibility, disempowering the studied population, and parroting the researcher. — [Workshop site](https://sites.google.com/andrew.cmu.edu/chi-llm-simulacra/home); [ACM extended abstract](https://dlnext.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772363.3778756)
- Co-Data at CHI 2026 asked for 2–4 page empirical studies of humans and LLMs doing collaborative data work (collection, cleaning, annotation, querying), including trust and equity. Deadline 19 February 2026. — [Co-Data site](https://codata.design/)
- Hugging Face’s 13 August 2026 blog describes a 15 July–2 August 2026 community hackathon that tried to reproduce ICML 2026 claims. Reported scale: 1,221 participants, 6,816 logbooks, 2,226 papers attempted (about 34% of the conference), 35,908 claims judged, 2,962 Hugging Face jobs, and $20 of compute credits per participant. An automated judge (GLM-5.2) labeled claims verified, falsified, toy, or inconclusive. The blog says 51% of examined papers (1,103) had at least one verified claim, 266 were fully reproduced, 632 were partial with nothing falsified, 3,978 claims were confirmed, 23% (496) had at least one falsified or contested claim, 49 had all claims falsified, and 242 had independent teams reaching opposite verdicts on the same claim. Participants were told to run toy reproductions when data were proprietary or checkpoints were unreleased. ICML 2026 received 23,918 submissions and accepted 6,352 papers, according to the same post. These figures are from a company blog about an automated judge, not from a peer-reviewed meta-analysis. — [Hugging Face blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/icml-2026-open-reproductions); [challenge page](https://huggingface.co/ICML-2026-agent-repro)
- Main-conference and journal papers already instantiate the contribution types, which is stronger evidence than workshop calls alone: Sun et al. is a negative result on mitigation (ICML 2025); Chen et al. is an evaluation survey with design principles (EMNLP 2025); Zhu et al. is an error analysis of benchmarks (preprint); Zhao et al. is a one-GPU systems measurement (ICPP 2026); Corradini et al. is a journal synthesis of small-model failure modes (2025).

### Inferences
- Contribution types that venues have actually written down as acceptable, and that fit one GPU, are: negative results with a mechanism, replications with an extra ablation, data and metric audits, checklist-style error analyses, small-model evaluations under a shared harness, human agreement studies, and single-device systems measurements.
- A new small-model training method is still possible, but it is the contribution type most likely to be out-scoped. Gupta et al.’s 7B-on-one-4090 remark is about a reported fine-tuning setup, not a license to pretrain. Prefer LoRA or post-training quantization on an existing open checkpoint, or skip training and evaluate.
- Human studies are in scope at CHI workshops and are named by Chen et al. as needed validation for dynamic benchmarks. They add ethics review, recruitment, and small sample size as the main constraints, not GPUs.
- The ICML 2026 hackathon shows that many papers can be probed with a toy budget, and that “verified” and “falsified” disagree across teams. A student replication should expect the original paper’s data or checkpoints to be missing and should plan a toy protocol in advance. It should not treat the blog’s 23% figure as a human-audited reproducibility rate.
- Workshop culture and TMLR policy are not the same as a main-conference oral. ICBINB, Insights, and HEAL are explicit homes for modest work. EMNLP main and ICML main accepted surveys and a large negative study, but those papers were broad (10 models, 20 strategies, a field survey). A first paper should aim at TMLR or a future workshop, and treat Findings or a main conference as a reach if the negative result is sharp and the sample is large enough to support the claim.

### Gaps
- No count was found of how many 2025 or 2026 main-conference papers were replications, data audits, or human studies. Acceptance is shown by policy text and by examples, not by a rate.
- No 2027 call was retrieved, so it is unknown whether Insights, ICBINB, HEAL, or Efficient Reasoning will run again and with what deadline.
- Whether ACL Rolling Review in early 2027 is a realistic path was not checked.
- The Hugging Face judge model and logbook dataset were not independently audited. Opposite verdicts on 242 papers may reflect judge noise, agent noise, or real scientific disagreement. The blog does not separate those causes.
- “Small-model methods” as a category is well represented in surveys, but this pass did not retrieve a 2026 proceedings paper that introduces a new architecture trained only on one consumer GPU and is accepted at a main conference. Absence of that example is not evidence it never happens.

## Eight to twelve specific topic sketches, each with a one-sentence claim a paper could test

### Takeaway
Ten sketches below are startable from public artifacts that 2025 papers already released. Each claim is a proposed hypothesis, not a result. The claim is useful only where the cited paper left a hole that a new experiment can fill. Several tempting agent and systems papers describe holes that are real but too large for the constraint.

### Cited Findings
The sketches rest on the findings in the previous sections. Additional constraints used while filtering:
- SWE-bench Pro V2 was announced by Scale as a refreshed public split with a network-locked evaluation protocol, co-developed with Reflection. The retrieved page is a product blog dated in the search snippet as a current release; the full post was not re-read line by line. Full agent runs of SWE-bench-style suites are a poor fit for a first paper even when the split is public. — [Scale Labs blog](https://labs.scale.com/blog/swe-bench-pro-v2)
- MemoryAgentBench’s solvable short-context regime versus its failed long-context regime is documented in the same paper (o4-mini 80.0 at 6K versus 14.0 at 32K on FactConsolidation multi-hop; at most 7% for the methods in the main multi-hop comparison). — [arXiv HTML 2507.05257v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05257v2)
- Official HealthBench-style medical scoring and GDPval-style expert pairwise judging were described on a secondary benchmark tracker (physician rubrics and expert raters; GPT-4.1 as a grader for HealthBench). That tracker is not the paper of record, so those details are not used as numbers. They only motivate keeping medical and economic evals off the sketch list unless the student collects their own small, non-clinical labels. — tracker page [alloevil.github.io/llm-benchmarks-tracker](https://alloevil.github.io/llm-benchmarks-tracker/) was not treated as a primary source for scores.

### Inferences
Proposed sketches. Venues assume a start date of 25 October 2026. “Plausible venue” means a venue whose written scope matches the contribution. It does not mean the 2026 workshop is still open.

1. **Language models — a contamination mitigation that does not destroy the task.** Claim: On one public benchmark and three open models that fit in 24 GB, a new semantic-preserving rewrite increases Sun et al.’s contamination resistance over the unmodified test set without dropping their fidelity metric below the range of the twenty strategies those authors already rejected. Why it is still open: Sun et al. (ICML 2025) found that none of 20 strategies achieved that balance. Evidence that would count: the same fidelity and resistance definitions, a preregistered rewrite rule, and a comparison against the unmodified set and against at least one published rewrite, on models that were not the whole point of the original paper. Venue: TMLR, or a future negative-results or evaluation workshop. If the new rule also fails, Insights-style venues want that negative result, but the 2026 Insights deadline has passed. Main risk: the original paper already searched this space; a small syntactic variant will look incremental, and a failure with no new mechanism will not clear TMLR’s “someone would care” bar.

2. **Language models — head-to-head small models under one harness.** Claim: When 1B-class, 3B-class, and 7–8B open instruction models are scored on the same public items with the same decoder settings, the rank order on exact-match math reverses under a continuous metric such as token-level log probability or partial credit, while the rank order on short factual questions does not. Why it is still open: Gupta et al. say current SLM-versus-LLM comparisons are cross-paper, that exact-match metrics can distort gaps (they cite Schaeffer et al.), and that they did not run a controlled comparison themselves. Evidence: one public code harness, all three size classes, at least two task families, and both an exact-match and a graded metric, with contamination risk stated rather than solved. Venue: TMLR or a future evaluation workshop. Main risk: by late 2026 a leaderboard may already have published this comparison; the paper fails if it only reprints a public leaderboard.

3. **Language models — multilingual gap at small scale.** Claim: Translating the same public question set into two non-English languages widens the accuracy gap between a sub-3B open model and a 7–8B open model more on multi-step items than on short factual items, and a native-speaker audit of a few hundred translations shows the gap is not explained by translation errors alone. Why it is still open: Gupta et al. say multilingual SLM evaluation is insufficient because most benchmarks are English. Evidence: fixed items, human spot-check of translations, per-language and per-item-type deltas, and a control that swaps the translator. Venue: *CL Findings or a future Insights-like workshop; TMLR if the audit changes the conclusion. Main risk: translationese and tokenizer fertility can fake a reasoning gap. Also unknown whether 2026 multilingual leaderboards already measured this exact contrast.

4. **Language models — safety behavior of models that actually run locally.** Claim: On a public safety or hallucination probe small enough to grade by hand, a 7B open model that looks close to a larger open model on MMLU-style accuracy refuses or abstains less often, and the errors concentrate in a few prompt families rather than uniformly. Why it is still open: Gupta et al. call SLM safety evaluation underdeveloped; the IEEE 2026 abstract names safety alignment under resource constraints; Corradini et al. find hallucinations stated as a challenge in 20% of the SLM papers they coded. Evidence: a few hundred hand-labeled items, inter-annotator agreement, and a comparison against accuracy on a standard knowledge set using the same checkpoints. Venue: TMLR or a future human-centered or negative-results workshop. Main risk: “refuses less” can be a decoding or prompt artifact. A medical or biosecurity probe would also pull the project into a domain this note is not recommending.

5. **Agents — selective forgetting at a context length that fits one GPU.** Claim: On the public 6K-token FactConsolidation multi-hop split, a memory policy that stores each fact once and overwrites it when a later turn contradicts it beats a fixed-chunk RAG baseline by a margin larger than run-to-run variation, for one open model in the 7–8B range. Why it is still open: Hu et al. say selective forgetting is unsolved for the agents they tested (at most 7% on multi-hop in the main comparison) and explicitly left “evaluate more agents” as future work. The 6K split is solvable in principle, because o4-mini scored 80 there, so the scientific question is whether a simple overwrite policy is enough for an open model at that length. Evidence: the released dataset, the RAG baseline reimplemented, the overwrite policy, and multiple seeds. Venue: TMLR or a future agent or evaluation workshop. Main risk: the 7% figure may not apply to the 6K split. If RAG already scores well at 6K, the claim dies. The 32K and 262K splits are the more interesting failure regime and may not fit the hardware constraint (see the next section).

6. **Agents — a validity audit that does not run a frontier agent.** Claim: Applying Zhu et al.’s outcome-validity and task-validity checks to one public, non-security agent benchmark finds at least one automatic grader that marks a hand-built trivial behavior as success, and publishing the patched grader changes the score of a single open agent by a stated amount. Why it is still open: Zhu et al. report grader bugs worth up to 100% relative error, including a 38% success rate for an empty τ-bench agent and a 5.2-point WebArena string-match inflation, and they say reporting practices were weak on every benchmark they scored. Evidence: the trivial behavior, the patched check, and before/after scores on one open agent or, if running the agent is too costly, before/after scores on the benchmark’s own logged trajectories if those logs are public. Venue: TMLR; this is the contribution type Insights 2026 described as metric and pipeline audits. Main risk: the specific bug may already have been fixed after the preprint. Re-finding a known τ-bench empty-response bug, without a new benchmark or a new quantitative consequence, is a failed paper. Do not choose CVE-Bench or any exploit suite.

7. **Agents — where stateful tool use breaks for one open model.** Claim: On a public subset of DialogTool, a single open model that fits one GPU loses more accuracy between early and late turns in tool execution than in tool selection, and the late-turn errors are mostly stale arguments rather than wrong tool names. Why it is still open: Wang et al. say that after 13 models, long-horizon stateful tool use is still poor, but they do not, in the abstract, isolate which stage collapses for a model a student can run. Evidence: stage-wise accuracy by turn, an error codebook with a second annotator, and a comparison to a stateless single-turn control. Venue: future agent workshop or TMLR. Main risk: VirtualMobile and the full six-task suite may be tedious to reproduce, and the abstract does not state that the data are sufficient to rerun every stage offline. If the released artifact is incomplete, the project stalls. Unknown as of this note.

8. **Efficiency — what 4-bit quantization actually costs, in joules and in errors.** Claim: For one open 7–8B model on one consumer GPU, 4-bit weight quantization changes short-form question accuracy by a small, stated band and changes multi-step math or long-context retrieval by a larger band, and joules per correct answer improve only on the task family where accuracy holds. Why it is still open: the IEEE 2026 abstract asks for standardized energy-aware benchmarks; Corradini et al. list scalability as a stated challenge in 19% of SLM papers; Gupta et al. say long-context SLM behavior past 32K is not competitive. The feasible claim stops at a context length that fits the card. Evidence: paired BF16-or-8-bit versus 4-bit runs, a power meter or on-device energy counter with the method named, and the same prompts. Venue: future efficiency workshop (the NeurIPS 2025 Efficient Reasoning call explicitly wanted commodity-GPU and cost measurements) or TMLR. Main risk: accuracy-versus-quantization is crowded. The paper is only interesting if the energy metric changes the decision of which checkpoint to deploy. Consumer-GPU energy counters can be noisy; a result that depends on one laptop’s power sensor may not generalize.

9. **Efficiency and systems — replicate the prefill/decode split on a card a student owns.** Claim: On one consumer GPU (about 16–24 GB) and one dense open model that fits, increasing batch size saturates prefill throughput while per-token decode latency stays memory-bandwidth bound, and a change that speeds the prefill kernels does not speed decode by the same factor. Why it is still open: Zhao et al. (ICPP 2026) give a cross-layer account on one A100 80GB for dense and MoE models, and they present the lack of this attribution as the motivation. A consumer-GPU replication is a different hardware claim, which is what their single-datacenter-GPU study does not settle. Evidence: vLLM or an equivalent server, TTFT versus decode latency, a simple profiler, and a documented GPU model. Venue: a systems workshop or TMLR reproducibility track, not MLSys main unless the measurement method itself is new. Main risk: the qualitative prefill/decode split is already textbook. The paper fails if it only redraws a known curve. It works if a specific kernel or quantization advice from the A100 paper reverses on the consumer card.

10. **Applied ML — when a small or classical model matches a time-series foundation model.** Claim: On a public univariate forecasting collection, a seasonal statistical baseline matches or beats a small open time-series model that can be fine-tuned on one GPU, and the ranking flips on a multivariate subset with covariates. Why it is still open: the NeurIPS 2025 workshop states that OpenTS, GIFT-Eval, and FoundTS already disagree on this point, and that full fine-tuning is still required for foundation models to compete. A student can arbitrate one slice instead of training a new foundation model. Evidence: the named public dataset, the statistical baseline, one small open model, and a preregistered split into univariate versus multivariate. Venue: a future time-series or evaluation workshop, or TMLR. Main risk: the workshop’s summary of those three benchmarks was not re-checked against the original papers in this pass. If the chosen dataset is one where the ranking is already stable and published, the claim is not open.

11. **Evaluation — human disagreement with a small judge model.** Claim: On a few hundred model answers to public questions, a locally run open judge in the 7–8B range agrees with two human raters about as often as the raters agree with each other on fluency, and agrees less on factual support, with disagreements concentrated on partial-credit items. Why it is still open: Insights 2026 explicitly invited demonstrations that metrics, including LLM-as-judge, block fair comparison; HEAL invited in-progress human audits; Chen et al. name human validation as future work for dynamic benchmarks; Gupta et al. name “LLMs as judges” as an evaluation gap for SLMs. Evidence: items, rubric, two raters, agreement statistics, and the judge’s raw decisions. Venue: TMLR or a future HCI or negative-results workshop. CHI 2026 workshops themselves are closed. Main risk: the result is prompt-sensitive, and a sample of a few hundred items supports a narrow claim only. Over-generalizing to “LLM judges don’t work” will not survive review.

12. **Evaluation and data — a public instruction or preference set contains a measurable bad slice.** Claim: A stratified manual audit of a few hundred examples from one widely used public instruction or preference dataset finds a stated rate of duplicated evaluation items, mislabeled preferences, or unusable text, and retraining a LoRA on the cleaned subset with a model that fits one GPU moves a downstream score by more than the seed variance. Why it is still open: Insights 2026 asked for audits of widely used data pipelines; Corradini et al. found data availability named in 21% of SLM papers; Chen et al. treat train-test overlap as a live evaluation failure. Evidence: the sample size, the codebook, a second coder on a subset, the contamination check against a named test set, and the before/after LoRA. Venue: TMLR or a future data-centric workshop. Main risk: the chosen dataset may already have a published audit, or the bad-slice rate may be too small to move the downstream number. Either outcome should be reported only if the audit design was fixed first. A negative audit is publishable at a negative-results venue and weaker at TMLR unless it overturns a common cleaning recipe.

Sketches that were considered and dropped as primary recommendations:
- Full UltraHorizon or 262K MemoryAgentBench runs. The papers define the hard regime at 35K–200K tokens and dozens to hundreds of tool calls.
- CVE-Bench or any offensive-security agent. Zhu et al. use it as a checklist case study; it is out of scope for this note.
- Pretraining a new SLM, even a small one, to chase Corradini et al.’s generalization numbers. Data curation at that scale is a different project from a 3–6 month first paper.
- Official HealthBench or GDPval leaderboard runs. They depend on large expert rubrics and, in secondary descriptions, a strong grader model. A student-scale human audit is sketch 11, not a medical leaderboard.

### Gaps
- No primary “future work” section was retrieved for a 2025–2026 multimodal model paper with a small-VLM experiment a student can rerun. Sketch 4 and Gupta et al.’s call for multimodal SLM evaluation are the closest sourced gaps. A chart-QA sketch would need a dataset check that this pass did not do. Unknown: which public chart or document benchmarks are unsaturated for open 2B–7B vision-language models in October 2026.
- DialogTool’s license, download size, and whether VirtualMobile can be run offline were not verified.
- It is unknown which of sketches 1–3 were already posted as preprints between the source papers and 25 October 2026.
- BabyLM-style small-data challenges were not re-checked for a 2025 or 2026 edition.

## What compute, data, and skills does each sketch actually require? What secretly needs a cluster or proprietary data?

### Takeaway
Sketches 1–4, 8, 11, and the audit half of 6 and 12 fit one consumer GPU or CPU plus public data, if the student uses existing checkpoints and a few hundred to a few thousand examples. Sketches 5, 7, 9, and 10 fit only on a carefully cut subset. The full versions of the agent and systems papers that motivate them need long context, many closed models, a datacenter GPU, or a kernel stack that this note’s hardware budget does not include.

### Cited Findings
Hardware and data facts used to flag the sketches:
- Sun et al. used 10 LLMs and 5 benchmarks. Their code is public. A student subset does not need all 10, but then the claim cannot be “across all benchmarks,” which is the scope of their negative result. — [PMLR 267](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/sun25t.html)
- Gupta et al. state that reported 7B training with 8-bit Adam fits an RTX 4090 24 GB, and that open challenges begin past 32K context. They also say frontier head-to-heads were not run. — [arXiv HTML 2501.05465v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05465v2)
- MemoryAgentBench contexts are constructed at 6K, 32K, 64K, and 262K. The authors say budget constrained them to a subset of agents, including commercial memory systems. — [arXiv HTML 2507.05257v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05257v2)
- DialogTool’s evaluation in the abstract used 13 open and closed models plus a virtual mobile environment. — [arXiv:2505.13328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13328)
- UltraHorizon’s heavy trajectories average 200K+ tokens and 400+ tool calls; standard configurations still exceed 35K tokens and 60 tool calls. — [arXiv PDF 2509.21766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.21766)
- Zhao et al. used one A100 80GB, 504 GB RAM, and Nsight Compute, including MoE models in BF16 under vLLM. — [ICPP 2026 abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3832810.3832894)
- The MLSys 2026 FlashInfer contest evaluation instructions specify a bare-metal NVIDIA B200 with locked clocks. That contest is not a one-consumer-GPU project. — [FlashInfer evaluation notes](https://github.com/flashinfer-ai/flashinfer-bench-starter-kit/blob/main/EVALUATION.md)
- Zhu et al. name SWE-bench Verified, τ-bench, SWE-Lancer, KernelBench, WebArena, and CVE-Bench. KernelBench is about generated GPU kernels. WebArena depends on live or replicated websites. CVE-Bench is offensive security. — [arXiv:2507.02825](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02825)
- The ICML 2026 reproduction blog says proprietary datasets and unreleased checkpoints forced toy reproductions, and that participants used hosted jobs plus coding agents. — [Hugging Face blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/icml-2026-open-reproductions)
- Human-subjects workshops (HEAL, Co-Data, LLM simulacra) describe interviews, surveys, and rater studies. They do not provide an ethics-review path for an independent researcher. — [HEAL](https://heal-workshop.github.io/); [ACM simulacra abstract](https://dlnext.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772363.3778756)

### Inferences
Requirements by sketch. “Secret blow-up” means a version of the idea that breaks the one-GPU, public-data, 3–6 month constraint even though the modest version does not.

1. **Contamination rewrite.** Compute: inference only, one GPU for 7–8B models, or a cloud GPU for a few days. CPU is enough for sub-1B models and for scoring cached logits. Data: one public benchmark plus Sun et al.’s public code. Skills: evaluation harnesses, basic statistics, careful dataset versioning. Secret blow-up: rerunning all 10 original LLMs, including API-only frontier models, or trying to retrain a model after the rewrite. Neither is required to test one new rewrite.

2. **Head-to-head harness.** Compute: one GPU; three open checkpoints; no training. Data: public test sets only. Skills: prompting controls, metric definitions, contamination literacy. Secret blow-up: adding a closed frontier model “for the real comparison.” That adds API cost and a moving target, and it is not required to answer Gupta et al.’s cross-paper complaint among open models. Also a blow-up: treating a full MMLU-plus-MATH-plus-long-context suite as mandatory. A few thousand items across two task families is the paper.

3. **Multilingual gap.** Compute: same as sketch 2, times two or three languages, still one GPU. Data: a public parallel set, or a machine translation plus a human audit. Skills: at least one non-English language in the author team or a paid native-speaker rater; agreement stats. Secret blow-up: building a new multilingual benchmark from scratch, or evaluating every language in a 50-language suite. Unknown: cost of native-speaker raters in the student’s country. Not a cluster issue.

4. **Local safety probe.** Compute: one GPU for generation; grading by hand or by a second open model that is then audited. Data: a few hundred public or self-written prompts. Skills: annotation guidelines, inter-annotator agreement, and enough safety-domain literacy to avoid confusing refusal with accuracy. Secret blow-up: red-teaming a frontier model, collecting real user logs, or using private incident data. Those are proprietary or out of scope. Keep prompts public and non-operational.

5. **Selective forgetting.** Compute: one GPU if the split is the 6K FactConsolidation subset and the model is a quantized 7–8B. Data: the authors’ released set, if the license allows reuse; this was not re-checked. Skills: RAG indexing, prompt controls, error analysis. Secret blow-up: the 64K–262K splits, long-context baselines, and the commercial memory agents (MIRIX, MemGPT-class systems) in the original paper. A 7B model at 262K tokens does not fit a 24 GB card in ordinary inference. The 32K setting is borderline and was not measured here; treat it as unknown until a memory estimator is run. Reproducing o4-mini’s 80-versus-14 curve requires that closed model.

6. **Benchmark validity audit.** Compute: often CPU-only if the student inspects graders and replays public trajectories. One GPU only if they rerun one open agent on a small task subset. Data: the benchmark’s public tests and, ideally, released logs. Skills: reading evaluation code, writing a trivial counterexample agent, basic experimental hygiene. Secret blow-up: SWE-bench Pro V2 or any network-locked, container-heavy software-engineering leaderboard; WebArena if the websites must be rehosted; KernelBench if the claim requires generating and fuzzing kernels on a datacenter GPU; CVE-Bench for scope reasons as well as compute. τ-bench’s empty-response bug can be demonstrated without a cluster, but it may already be fixed.

7. **Stateful tool use.** Compute: one GPU for one open model on a turn-limited subset. Data: DialogTool, only if the full release is public. Skills: dialogue evaluation, error coding. Secret blow-up: the abstract’s 13-model matrix, closed models, and VirtualMobile if it needs a phone emulator plus live APIs. Those were not costed. If the dataset is not actually downloadable, this sketch is not startable. That availability is unknown.

8. **Energy and quantization.** Compute: one consumer or single cloud GPU that exposes a power interface; several repeated runs. Data: public prompts. Skills: quantization tooling (a standard 4-bit format), power measurement, paired statistics. Secret blow-up: training-time energy, MoE models that do not fit, or claiming device-independent joules from one laptop. The IEEE abstract’s “standardized energy-aware benchmark” could become a large engineering project; the paper should measure one model family, not propose a new community standard and also populate it.

9. **Consumer-GPU prefill/decode.** Compute: one 16–24 GB GPU, dense model only (for example a quantized 7–8B, or a smaller dense model in higher precision). Data: a public prompt mix with varied lengths. Skills: serving stack, profiling, not writing a new CUDA kernel. Secret blow-up: copying Zhao et al.’s MoE-plus-BF16-plus-A100-80GB-plus-Nsight design. MoE BF16 on an A100 is not the consumer-GPU study. FlashInfer-Bench on a B200 is a different, non-modest project. Nsight-level kernel work is a skills blow-up more than a cluster blow-up, and it is unnecessary for the TTFT-versus-decode claim.

10. **Time-series baselines.** Compute: CPU for statistical baselines; one GPU only if the open time-series model is fine-tuned. Many classical baselines need no GPU. Data: whichever of GIFT-Eval, OpenTS, or FoundTS is public and licensed for research. This pass did not confirm licenses or sizes. Skills: forecasting metrics, leakage-safe splits, and reading the original benchmark papers before trusting the workshop summary. Secret blow-up: pretraining a time-series foundation model, or using an industrial dataset that the workshop mentioned only as motivation. Unknown: parameter count and fine-tune memory of the particular open model chosen.

11. **Human judge study.** Compute: one GPU or a CPU for a small judge; the bottleneck is rater hours. Data: a few hundred public answers, or answers the student generates from an open model. Skills: rubric design, two raters, agreement metrics, and a plan for ethics review. Secret blow-up: paying a crowd to imitate HealthBench’s physician panel, or using a proprietary judge as the ground truth. An independent researcher may not have an institutional review board. Whether a journal will accept the study without one is unknown. The simulacra workshop’s warning is the design constraint: do not replace the human raters with another LLM and then claim a human result.

12. **Data audit plus LoRA.** Compute: CPU for the audit; one GPU for a short LoRA on a 7–8B or smaller model. Data: one public instruction or preference corpus and one public downstream set. Skills: sampling, a written codebook, deduplication, and enough training skill to keep the fine-tune from becoming the whole project. Secret blow-up: auditing a private web-scale pretraining corpus, or full fine-tuning rather than LoRA. Pretraining-corpus audits need data the student does not have. Also a blow-up: datasets whose license forbids redistribution of an audited subset; the paper can still report rates without republishing the text, but that must be checked per corpus. Not checked here for a specific dataset.

Across all twelve, the skills that actually decide whether the paper finishes are evaluation design, annotation, and statistics. New CUDA, new pretraining, distributed systems, and closed-model access are the skills and resources that turn a feasible sketch into a lab project.

Calendar fit: a 3–6 month project started on 25 October 2026 ends between late January and late April 2027. TMLR is rolling and is the only venue in this note with an open, written policy that matches that window. Workshop dates in 2027 were not found. ICLR 2026’s own deadline (abstract 19 September 2025, paper 24 September 2025) shows that ICLR-style deadlines fall about six months before the meeting; the analogous 2027 date was not retrieved and must not be assumed.

### Gaps
- No measurement was run of 7B-model memory at 8K, 32K, or 64K on a 24 GB card under a named inference engine. Fit claims above 6K are inferences, not experiments.
- Licenses and actual download availability were not checked for MemoryAgentBench, DialogTool, GIFT-Eval, OpenTS, FoundTS, or a specific instruction corpus.
- Rater costs, IRB options for independent researchers, and cloud-GPU prices as of October 2026 were not looked up.
- SWE-bench Pro V2’s hardware and time cost were not measured. The network-locked description is enough to flag a full run as a poor first project, not enough to quote a GPU-hour number.
- ProfInfer (MLSys 2026) was fetched as a PDF but its hardware requirements were not extracted. It is not evidence for or against a single-consumer-GPU profiler paper.
